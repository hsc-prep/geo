Q16: Skills Practice

In the exam, Q16 (and often several of Q1–15) draws heavily on the Geographical Skills Continuum — maps, graphs and images — alongside content questions. Practise the MC bank and the Written Skills bank below, then use the construction guides to make sure you can actually build these graphs and maps by hand, not just read them.

Construction Geo-Skills

Six graph/map types you may be asked to construct, complete or interpret. Work through each guide, then practise drawing one from scratch on paper — construction questions are marked on technique (axes, labels, scale, accuracy), not just the finished picture.

Climate graphs

A compound graph showing a place's average monthly rainfall (bars) and average monthly temperature (line) on the same set of axes.

  1. Draw a horizontal axis with the 12 months (Jan–Dec) evenly spaced.
  2. Draw a left-hand vertical axis for rainfall (mm), starting at 0, scaled to fit the highest monthly rainfall value.
  3. Draw a right-hand vertical axis for temperature (°C), scaled to fit the full temperature range.
  4. Plot each month's rainfall as a bar (usually blue) up to the correct height on the rainfall axis.
  5. Plot each month's mean temperature as a point on the temperature axis, then join the 12 points with a smooth line (usually red).
  6. Add a title (place name), axis labels with units, and a key distinguishing rainfall bars from the temperature line.
  7. If asked, calculate total annual rainfall (sum of all bars) and the annual temperature range (highest minus lowest monthly mean).
Exam tip: Always check which axis is which before reading values off a given climate graph — rainfall and temperature scales rarely start at the same maximum.

Population pyramids

A back-to-back horizontal bar graph showing the age-sex structure of a population.

  1. Draw a central vertical axis and label 5-year age cohorts (0–4, 5–9, 10–14 …) from youngest at the bottom to oldest at the top.
  2. Draw a horizontal axis on each side of the centre line: males on the left, females on the right, scaled in either population numbers or percentage of total population.
  3. For each age cohort, draw a horizontal bar extending left (male) and right (female) to the correct value.
  4. Add a title (place name and year), axis labels, and units (numbers or %).
  5. Describe the overall shape: a wide base and narrow top = expansive (high birth rate, young population, common in developing countries); roughly even sides = stationary (low birth and death rates, ageing developed country); a narrowing base = constrictive (declining birth rate).
Exam tip: Never just describe individual bars — examiners want the overall shape and what it reveals about birth rate, death rate and dependency ratio.

Ternary (triangular) graphs

A triangular graph used to plot three variables that add up to 100% at once — e.g. employment by primary/secondary/tertiary sector, or soil composition (sand/silt/clay).

  1. Each of the triangle's three sides is a 0–100% axis for one variable; the three axes always sum to 100% at any point inside the triangle.
  2. To plot a value: find the correct value on each of the three axes, then draw a line parallel to the appropriate side of the triangle from each value — the point where the three lines meet is your data point.
  3. To read a point already on the graph, draw lines from the point parallel to each side back to each axis and read off the three values (they should sum to 100%, allowing for rounding).
  4. Label each axis clearly with the variable it represents, and add a title.
Exam tip: Practise reading a given point before trying to plot one — ternary graphs are usually tested as a reading/interpretation skill first.

Cross sections

A side-on profile of the land surface along a chosen line on a topographic map, showing how relief changes with distance.

  1. Draw a straight line on the map between your two chosen points (e.g. A to B).
  2. Lay the straight edge of a piece of paper along this line and mark a tick wherever a contour line crosses the edge, noting its height.
  3. Transfer the paper to a sheet of graph paper: draw a horizontal axis (distance) and vertical axis (altitude, in metres) with a sensible, evenly-spaced scale.
  4. Mark each tick at its correct horizontal position and correct height, then join the points with a smooth curved line following the shape of the land.
  5. Add a title, axis labels and units, and note the vertical exaggeration (vertical scale is almost always exaggerated compared to the horizontal scale — state the ratio).
  6. Label key features on the profile (e.g. river valley, ridge, saddle) that correspond to features on the map.
Exam tip: State the vertical exaggeration whenever you draw or interpret a cross section — it makes slopes look steeper than they really are, and examiners reward you for noting this.

Transects

A line drawn across an area (on a map or in the field) along which data is recorded at regular intervals, then plotted as a profile.

  1. Choose a transect line that crosses the feature you want to investigate (e.g. a change in vegetation, land use, or a beach profile).
  2. Record data at regular intervals along the line (e.g. every 10 or 20 metres): this might be vegetation type/height, land use, slope angle, or another variable depending on the investigation.
  3. Plot the data as a profile diagram: distance along the transect on the horizontal axis, the measured variable (height, elevation, land use category) on the vertical axis.
  4. Annotate the profile with labels identifying zones or changes (e.g. 'foredune vegetation', 'residential land use').
  5. Add a title, axis labels, units and, where relevant, the date and location of the fieldwork.
Exam tip: A transect is really a cross section with fieldwork data plotted on it instead of contour heights — the construction method is almost identical.

Précis (sketch) maps

A simplified, hand-drawn version of a topographic or other map, showing only the features relevant to a particular question.

  1. Draw a frame (usually a rectangle) that keeps the same proportions as the original map so your sketch stays roughly to scale.
  2. Lightly mark the four corner grid references of your frame so the sketch can be matched back to the original map.
  3. Using a ruler, transfer the main features required by the question only (e.g. relief, drainage, settlement, communication lines) — leave out irrelevant detail.
  4. Use appropriate symbols, shading or hachures for relief, and label key features (place names, rivers, roads) clearly.
  5. Add a title, a north point, an approximate scale, and a key/legend explaining any symbols used.
Exam tip: A précis map is marked on accuracy of proportion and completeness against the question's instructions, not artistic quality — always include title, scale, north point and key.
